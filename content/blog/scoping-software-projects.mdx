Most software projects do not fail during the build. They fail during the
conversation that happened three weeks earlier, when nobody wanted to be the
person who said "we do not actually know how long this takes yet."

## The problem with hourly

Hourly billing looks fair. You pay for time spent, the vendor is not penalised
for a hard problem, everyone is aligned. In practice it moves all of the
estimation risk onto the client, and it does so silently.

The vendor has no financial reason to resolve ambiguity early. If a
requirement is vague, the cheapest thing to do is start building and find out.
Every hour of that discovery is billable. Nobody is being dishonest — the
incentive simply points away from the hard, unglamorous work of pinning things
down before code gets written.

## What we do instead

Every Codecrons engagement opens with a **paid discovery phase**, usually one
to two weeks. It is deliberately short and deliberately not free, because free
scoping gets treated as a sales call rather than real work.

The output is a document containing:

- The problem stated in the client's own language, not ours
- A list of what is explicitly **out** of scope
- The data model and the integration points
- A build plan broken into weekly increments
- A fixed price and a delivery date

That document is yours regardless of what happens next. If you take it to
another studio, or build it in-house, that is a legitimate outcome. We would
rather lose the build than deliver something we scoped badly.

## Where fixed scope actually breaks

It is worth being honest about the failure mode. Fixed scope goes wrong when
requirements genuinely change mid-build — not when they were unclear, but when
the business itself moved.

We handle that with a simple rule: **change requests are re-scoped, not
absorbed.** A new requirement gets its own estimate and its own decision. That
keeps the original number meaningful, and it keeps the conversation about
trade-offs rather than blame.

## The part clients notice

The unexpected benefit is not the price certainty. It is that discovery forces
the difficult conversations into week one, when changing your mind is cheap,
instead of week nine, when it is not.

Almost every project we scope gets *smaller* during discovery. Something that
felt essential turns out to be a nice-to-have, and cutting it takes three weeks
off the timeline. That conversation only happens if someone is paid to have it.
